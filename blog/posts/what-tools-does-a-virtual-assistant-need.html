What Tools Does a New Virtual Assistant Actually Need?

17 November 2026 · Virtueasy · Tools & Setup · 6 min read

Eight charges on the card, zero clients on the books. Here is the four step clear out that gets a new VA down to the tools year one actually needs, and keeps the pile from coming back.

Watch the full video, then keep reading for the breakdown.

Somewhere in the first month, most new VAs end up with a tool pile. Three project apps, two design tools, a scheduler, an AI writer, and a card statement showing eight small charges every month before a single client has paid an invoice. Nobody plans it. One video says every VA needs this app, a Facebook group swears by that one, and each signup felt like progress.

It is not progress. It is overhead, and it arrives before the income does. This post walks through the four step clear out from the video: sort everything into four categories, start free in each one, let the client pay for the client's tools, and run a short audit every month so nothing renews that you do not use. By the end, year one needs about four tools, and you know exactly why each one is there.

Step 1: Sort everything into four categories

Year one of VA work has four jobs that need software. Everything else is optional.

  • Talk. Your email and your video calls. This is where clients find you, brief you and hear back from you.
  • Files. Where documents live and how you share them. A client sends a spreadsheet, you send a draft back, and both of you can find it next month.
  • Time. Your calendar, and the way a client books a call with you without six emails about Tuesday versus Thursday.
  • Money. How you send an invoice and how the client pays it. If that part still feels fuzzy, the post on invoicing clients as a VA covers the whole loop.

Take every tool you currently pay for or log into and put it in one of those four boxes. One tool per box is enough. If something does not fit a box, it is a nice to have, and nice to haves are the first thing to go when there is no income yet to cover them.

Our example VA, Priya, had eleven tools when she tried this. Five fit a box at all. The other six were things she had been told she would need eventually. Eventually is not year one.

If you want a shortlist of specific apps for each box, the post on the tools most new VAs use names them. The point here is not which app. The point is that four boxes is the whole list.

Step 2: Start free, and pay only when real work hits the limit

In all four categories there is usually a free version that is plenty for your first few clients. A free email account sends email. A free calendar link books calls. A free file drive holds more documents than two clients will ever send you. A free invoicing tool sends an invoice and tells you when it is paid.

So the rule is simple: start free in every box. Upgrade only when the free version stops you doing work a client is waiting on. That phrase matters, so here is what it does not mean.

  • Not when the paid version looks more professional. Clients notice whether the work arrived, not which plan sent it.
  • Not when someone online says the paid one is better. It might be. You still do not need it this month.
  • Not when a feature list makes you nervous about what you might be missing.

Pay when a client is waiting on something and the free tier is the thing in the way. A file too large to share. A calendar that cannot offer two meeting lengths. A storage limit you actually hit. Those are real limits, and when one shows up, the upgrade pays for itself the same week.

In Priya's example, that one rule took her monthly tool spend from ninety dollars to twelve. Those are made up numbers, but the direction is the usual one. The free tiers were never the problem. The habit of paying early was.

Step 3: Know what the client should pay for

Here is where a lot of VAs quietly lose money. A client needs a project board, so the VA signs up for one. The client wants an email newsletter, so the VA opens an email marketing account. The client's social scheduler? Also on the VA's card. Then the engagement ends, and the VA is either paying for tools that belong to someone else's business or doing an awkward handover of accounts that were never hers to hold.

The test is one question: does this tool run my business, or theirs?

If a tool runs the client's business, it goes on the client's account, in the client's name, on the client's card. You get invited in as a user. That covers the obvious ones, like their project board, their email list, their social scheduler and their website host. It also covers the less obvious ones, like a paid stock photo subscription that only exists for their posts.

This is better for everyone, not just your bank balance.

  • Your costs stay down. Your four boxes are yours. Everything client specific is theirs.
  • The client keeps everything. When the work ends, nothing has to be exported, migrated or handed over. The board, the list and the scheduler were always in their account.
  • Access is cleaner. Being invited into a client's tool as a named user is safer than holding the master login, which is the same reason the post on handling client logins tells you never to accept a password in a chat message.

Say it out loud in onboarding. A line like "any tool that runs your business stays in your name, and you add me as a user" sounds organized, because it is. Most clients expect it. The ones who push back usually want you to carry a cost they do not want to see on their own statement, which tells you something about the engagement.

Step 4: Run a monthly subscription audit

Tools creep back. A free trial you forgot to cancel, a plan you upgraded for one project, an app you were sure you would use. The fix is a short audit, once a month, on a fixed day.

Here is the whole process.

  • Open your bank or card statement for the last month.
  • List every charge that renews. Monthly, annual, all of them.
  • Next to each one, write the last time you used it for paid work. Not for your own planning, not for a course, for work a client paid for.
  • Anything you have not used for paid work in thirty days gets canceled.

That last line is where people hesitate, so hold on to this: if you need it again, signing back up takes about two minutes. Canceling does not close a door. It stops a charge. The tool will still be there next month if real work shows up for it.

Priya does hers on the first of every month. It takes about ten minutes, and most months she cancels nothing, which is the point. The audit is not there to find a problem every time. It is there so the pile never comes back.

Two refinements once the habit is in place. Annual plans deserve a note of their renewal date in your calendar, because a thirty day rule will not catch a charge that only appears once a year. And once you have more than a couple of clients, the audit is a good moment to check that each client's tools are still on their card and not drifting onto yours.

Do this today: cancel one tool

You do not need to run the full audit this minute. The homework from the video is smaller and more useful: cancel one tool today. Just one.

Pick the easiest one. The trial you never used. The second project app. The design tool you opened twice. Cancel it, and notice that nothing about your work changes tomorrow. That is the proof the rest of the pile is built on the same logic.

Then, when you have a spare twenty minutes, do the sort. Four boxes: talk, files, time, money. One tool in each, free until real work hits the limit. Everything that runs a client's business goes on the client's card. And a ten minute audit on the first of the month keeps it that way.

Year one does not need a tech stack. It needs four tools and your first client. When you are ready for that part, the free Virtueasy job board is the place to start looking.
