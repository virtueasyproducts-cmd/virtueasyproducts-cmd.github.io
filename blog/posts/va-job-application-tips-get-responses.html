How to Apply to VA Job Listings So You Actually Hear Back

19 August 2026 · Virtueasy · Applications & Outreach · 6 min read

You sent the application. You wrote something like "I would love to help with your social media!" You waited. Nothing.

If that sounds familiar, the problem is almost never your skills. It is that your application asked the client to do work. They opened it, could not tell in ten seconds whether you understood their business, and moved on to the next one.

Most listings get dozens of replies that all look the same. The ones that get answered are not the most impressive. They are the easiest to say yes to.

Why Most Applications Get Skipped

The person posting a VA listing is usually overwhelmed. That is why they are hiring. They are not sitting down to carefully evaluate candidates. They are skimming a full inbox between other work, looking for a reason to stop skimming.

A generic application gives them no reason. "I have three years of experience and I am detail oriented" describes almost every applicant. So does "I would love to support your business." Neither one tells the client what changes for them if they hire you.

The second failure is length. A long application does not read as thorough. It reads as one more thing to get through, so it gets saved for later, which means never.

Read the Listing for the Job Behind the Job

Before you write anything, read the listing twice and separate what they asked for from what they actually need.

The stated ask is the task list: inbox management, scheduling, posting three times a week. The real need sits underneath it. Someone hiring for inbox management is drowning in email and losing track of things. Someone hiring for social posting has probably gone quiet for a month and feels behind.

Write down the specific words they used. If the listing says "I need someone who can just take this off my plate," that phrase tells you what to lead with. Clients respond when they hear their own problem described back to them in their own language.

Also look for what is missing. A listing with no hours, no rate, and no timeline usually means the client has not thought it through yet. That is not a reason to skip it. It is a reason to ask one good question, which most applicants will not do.

The Short Application That Gets a Reply

Four short paragraphs. That is the whole thing.

Open by naming their situation, not your credentials. One sentence that shows you read the listing and understood what is actually going on.

Second, say what you would do about it. Be specific and concrete. Not "I can help with your inbox" but "I would set up a triage system so anything needing you is in one folder by 9am and the rest is handled without you."

Third, one line of proof. The closest thing you have done to this, with a result if you have one. If you do not have a client example yet, use something you built or ran for yourself.

Fourth, make the next step easy. Offer a specific time or two for a short call, or ask the one question that moves things forward.

Then stop. Do not add a paragraph about your work ethic. Do not list every tool you have ever touched. The application is not your life story, it is a sample of what working with you feels like.

What to Attach and What to Leave Out

Attach one thing: a short portfolio link, or a one page overview of your services. One link, clearly labeled, that loads without asking anyone to sign in.

Leave out the resume unless they asked for one. A traditional resume is built for a hiring process that does not exist here. The client does not care about your job titles from six years ago, they care whether you can run their inbox next week.

Leave out long attachments, ZIP files, and anything that has to be downloaded. Every extra step costs you replies.

If you do not have a portfolio yet, that is fixable faster than you think, and it is worth doing before you send another twenty applications into silence.

Naming Your Rate Without Underselling

Most listings ask for your rate. Most VAs either dodge the question or name a number well under what they need, hoping it gets them picked.

Dodging reads as inexperience. Underpricing gets you hired by the clients who are hardest to work with, because the people shopping on price are usually the same ones who push scope.

Name a real number and frame it against outcomes, not hours. "My rate for ongoing inbox and calendar support is X per month for roughly Y hours" gives the client something they can actually evaluate. If your number lands above their stated budget, say so plainly and offer a smaller scope that fits it. That answer wins work more often than you would expect, because it shows you can think about their constraint instead of just absorbing it.

If you are not confident your number is right, settle that before you apply, not in the middle of a negotiation.

How Soon to Follow Up

Once, after five to seven business days.

Keep it to two sentences. Restate the one thing you would do for them, and confirm you are still available. Do not apologize for following up, do not ask whether they received it, and do not send a second one.

Silence after one follow up is an answer. The listing may have been filled, the client may have changed their mind, or the budget may have vanished. None of those are about you, and chasing them costs you the hours you could spend on the next application.

What Actually Moves the Number

Applying more is not the fix when the application is the problem. Ten targeted applications where you clearly read the listing will beat fifty copy pasted ones, and they take less total time.

Track what you send. Note the listing, the date, what you offered, and whether you heard back. After twenty applications you will see a pattern in which openings get replies, and that pattern is worth more than any template someone hands you.

Then the calls start, and that is a different skill.
