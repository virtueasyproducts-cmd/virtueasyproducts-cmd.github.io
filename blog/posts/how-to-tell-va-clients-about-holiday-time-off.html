How to Tell Your VA Clients About Holiday Time Off

25 November 2026 · Virtueasy · Client Communication · 6 min read

You are allowed to take the holidays off. The notice, the timing and a clear coverage plan are what turn a week away into a non-event for your clients.

You are allowed to take the holidays off.

That sentence probably needs to sit for a second, because if you have two to five retainer clients depending on you, the idea of going quiet for a week in December can feel like a risk. What if they see it as flaky? What if something comes up? What if they start wondering whether they need someone more "available"?

Here is the reality: clients who work with professional VAs expect planned time off. What they do not expect, and what actually shakes trust, is finding out on a Friday afternoon that you are gone until January. The notice, the timing, and a clear coverage plan are what turn a week off into a non-event.

This post walks you through all of it. When to tell your clients, what to say, how to handle coverage, how billing works for a short month, and how to come back clean.

When to Send the Notice

Three to four weeks out. Not two, and definitely not the week before.

If your break runs December 23 through January 1, your clients should hear from you by the end of November at the latest. That window gives them time to front-load any requests before you go, adjust their own plans if needed, and feel like they are working with someone organized, not someone scrambling.

Send it as a standalone message, not buried in a weekly update or a project thread. It deserves its own subject line.

What the Notice Actually Says

Four things. That is it.

  • The dates you will be out.
  • What you will complete before you go.
  • What happens if something urgent comes up while you are away.
  • The date you are back and available.

You do not need to explain why you are taking time off. You do not need to apologize for it. A short, matter-of-fact message signals confidence and professionalism far more than a long explanation does.

Here is a copy-ready version you can adapt:

Subject: My availability over the holidays

Hi [Client name],

Just a heads-up on my schedule for the end of the year. I will be out from [date] through [date] and back at my desk on [return date].

Before I go, I will make sure [specific recurring tasks] are completed and that [anything time-sensitive] is handled or handed off. If something genuinely urgent comes up while I am away, [what to do: a backup contact, or a note that it will wait until I am back].

Happy to push anything forward before I leave if the timing is useful for you. Just send it my way before [cutoff date].

[Your name]

Short. Specific. No apology. Done.

The Coverage Plan

Before you log off, do the actual work of reducing what could go wrong.

Front-load recurring tasks. Any weekly reporting, scheduling, inbox management, or social posts that fall inside your break window should be completed or queued before you leave. If you use a scheduler for content, fill the queue. If your client has a weekly check-in email they rely on, send it early.

Build a short handover doc. This does not need to be a 10-page manual. A one-page note covering any open projects, what stage each one is at, where the files live, and any decisions that might need to be made is enough. Leave it somewhere the client can access without asking you.

Decide what happens to true emergencies. You have two honest options. Option one is a backup VA you trust who can handle time-sensitive work at an agreed rate. If you go this route, make sure your client knows and has that person's contact. Option two is a clear statement that non-emergency items wait until you return, and that you will respond to anything flagged urgent within 24 hours of your return date. Either is professional. The mistake is saying nothing and leaving clients to guess.

Set an out-of-office reply that does the job. It should confirm you are out, give your return date, and either provide the backup contact or confirm that you will respond on your return date. Keep it short. One sentence per point.

How Billing Works for a Short Month

This is the part people skip over in the notice, and it is worth addressing directly.

If you are on a flat monthly retainer and you are taking a week off, you have three clean options.

  • Prorate the invoice for that month. Calculate your daily or weekly rate and reduce the invoice by the time you are out.
  • Keep the flat fee and bank the hours. You front-load the work, deliver everything the client expects, and the month is fully covered even though the calendar hours look uneven.
  • Keep the flat fee with no adjustment. This works if your retainer is scoped by deliverables rather than hours, and you are completing everything before you leave.

Whichever you choose, say it in the notice. Something as simple as "My December invoice will be prorated to reflect the week I am out" or "My retainer covers deliverables rather than hours, so December billing stays the same" removes any ambiguity.

If you are not sure how your contract reads on this, now is the time to check.

Your First Day Back

Do not go straight into client work without a plan. Give yourself an hour on your first morning to triage properly.

Scan for anything marked urgent. Answer anything that is a quick response. Flag anything that needs longer work and add it to your task list before you open a single project file. Send a brief check-in to each retainer client letting them know you are back and their work is moving.

That one hour on your first morning prevents you from spending your first week back reactive and behind.

Pre-Break Checklist

Work through this before you log off for the holiday.

  • Notice sent to all retainer clients three to four weeks before your break.
  • Recurring tasks front-loaded or queued for the full break window.
  • Open projects documented in a short handover note with file locations included.
  • Backup contact confirmed and shared with clients, or a clear "this waits" statement included in your notice.
  • Out-of-office reply set on email and any client communication tools.
  • December billing decision confirmed and stated in your notice.
  • Cutoff date communicated for pre-break requests.
  • Return date in your calendar with a first-morning triage block reserved.

Taking time off is not a threat to your client relationships. How you communicate it is what determines how clients receive it. Send the notice early, keep it specific, do the prep work, and your clients will see exactly what you want them to see: someone who runs their business well.

If you want to build a client communication system that holds up year-round, not just at the holidays, start with how you document your work and how you report on it.
