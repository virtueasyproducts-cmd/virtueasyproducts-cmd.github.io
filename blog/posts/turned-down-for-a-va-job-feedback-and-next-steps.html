Turned Down for a VA Job? How to Ask for Feedback and Turn It Into Your Next Yes

6 November 2026 · Virtueasy · Job Hunting · 6 min read

You did not get the VA job. Here is the two-sentence reply that keeps the door open, and the simple log that shows you what to fix before the next one.

Rejection is part of applying to VA listings. That is not a motivational line to make you feel better. It is just true, and if you are a few applications in with nothing to show for it, you need practical tools more than you need reassurance.

Most rejections arrive as a polite one-liner or not at all. No explanation, no notes, nothing to act on. That does not mean there is nothing to learn. It means you have to be strategic about how you collect information, because the pattern across your rejections will tell you far more than any single no.

This post covers how to ask for feedback professionally, how to read silence when nobody replies, how to track where you are losing so you know what to fix, and how to keep applying consistently through a stretch of noes.

Why Rejection Feels Like a Verdict (and Why It Isn't)

When you spend time tailoring a cover message, submitting samples, and maybe completing a test task, a rejection stings. It is easy to read it as a judgment on whether you belong in VA work at all.

It is not. It is one data point. A client's budget changed. The hire went to someone with a specific tool they forgot to list in the ad. The chosen applicant had one more year of experience in a niche the client decided mattered after they posted. You will never know unless you ask, and even then you may not find out. What you can control is how you use the information you do get and how you build up enough data points to see a real pattern.

The Two-Sentence Reply That Keeps the Door Open

When you receive a rejection, send a short, professional reply within 24 to 48 hours. Keep it to two sentences. Thank them and ask for one specific piece of feedback.

Here is a template you can copy and adjust:

Subject: Re: [Position Name] Application

"Hi [Name],

Thank you for letting me know. If you have a moment, I would genuinely appreciate one piece of feedback on my application or interview, as I am actively working to improve. Either way, I wish you well with the new hire.

[Your name]"

That is it. Two sentences of substance, no pressure, no long explanation of why you deserved the role. The brevity signals professionalism. It also leaves the door open, because sometimes the chosen hire falls through in the first two weeks, and a VA who responded gracefully is the first person a client thinks of.

Do not send this if the rejection came from a platform with automated messaging and no direct contact. Save it for listings where you had a real exchange with a human.

How to Read Silence

No reply to your application at all is data. It is not personal. It means something in the application itself is not converting, which is a solvable problem.

If you follow up after a rejection and get no response to your feedback request, that is fine. Most clients will not reply. You sent the message, you left a good impression, you move on. Do not follow up twice.

What you should not do is interpret silence as confirmation that you are not cut out for VA work. Silence means the application did not land. That is a craft problem, not a character problem.

Your One-Page Application Log

This is the most practical thing in this post. If you are applying without tracking, you are flying blind.

Set up a simple log, one row per application. You only need six columns:

  • Date applied
  • Client or listing name
  • Where you found it
  • Outcome
  • Stage you lost at
  • Notes

Under Outcome, use four options: No reply, Rejected after application, Rejected after interview, Rejected after test task.

Stage you lost at is where the pattern lives:

  • Mostly no replies: the problem is the application itself. Your cover message, your rate presentation, or how you are positioning your skills is not compelling enough to get a response. This is where to focus your energy first.
  • Interviews but no offers: the call is where you are losing. You are compelling on paper but something is breaking down in how you present yourself, price your services, or answer questions live.
  • Test tasks that do not convert: either the skill needs work or you are misreading the brief. Both are fixable, but they require different solutions.

Run this log for 20 applications before you draw conclusions. Ten is not enough to see a pattern, especially if you are applying to a wide range of listing types.

What to Change for Each Pattern

No replies: Rewrite your cover message. Lead with what you can do for the client, not your background. Check that your rate is in the right range for the listing. Look at whether you are applying to listings where you genuinely have the required skills.

No offer after the interview: Practice talking about your services out loud. Record yourself answering common questions like "what do you charge" and "what does your onboarding process look like." If you do not have a clear answer to those, the client will feel the uncertainty.

No conversion after test tasks: Read the brief again slowly before you decide the skill is the issue. Often the brief is vague and the VA who wins interprets it, asks a clarifying question, or formats the deliverable in a way that shows initiative. If the skill genuinely needs work, name it and go build it.

When to Stop Applying to a Type of Listing

If you have sent 15 or more applications to a specific type of listing, say entry-level social media VA roles, and your reply rate is under 10 percent, that listing type may not be the right fit for where you are right now. That does not mean leave VA work. It means pivot to a listing type where your skills are a stronger match and come back to that niche once you have more to show.

Keeping Your Weekly Target Steady

A run of rejections will test your consistency more than anything else. The fix is to treat your application target as a non-negotiable weekly output, not a goal that moves based on how you feel.

Set a number you can hit even in a hard week. For most newer VAs, five to eight quality applications a week is more sustainable than twenty rushed ones. Steady output over time produces results. Spikes followed by discouragement do not.

If feedback is hard to get and your log is showing a pattern you cannot crack on your own, that is a good moment to look at structured support.

What to Do Right Now

Pull up a blank spreadsheet and set up your application log before your next submission. Six columns, one row per application, filled in honestly. Give it 20 rows before you decide anything.

If you already have 20 applications in and a clear losing pattern, you know what to work on. Pick one thing, fix it, and keep your weekly target in place.

Rejection is not a verdict. It is information. Use it.
