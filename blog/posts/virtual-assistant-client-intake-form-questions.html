Virtual Assistant Client Intake Form: 18 Questions to Ask Before You Start Work

13 November 2026 · Virtueasy · Onboarding & Retention · 6 min read

A client intake form is the cheapest insurance a VA has. Eighteen questions, sent once, answer most of what you would otherwise chase over the first three weeks. Here they are, grouped by what each one protects you from.

Most first-month problems with a new client are not skill problems. They are missing-information problems. You did not know she hates being messaged after 6 p.m. You did not know the invoice goes to her bookkeeper and not to her. You did not know there was a second Canva account. None of that is hard to find out. It just has to be asked once, in writing, before the work starts.

That is what an intake form is for. It is not a personality quiz and it is not a discovery call in disguise. The client has already said yes. The form collects what you need to do the job without sending her eleven separate questions in week one.

When to Send It

After the contract is signed and the first invoice is paid, and before the kickoff call. Give her two or three business days. You want the answers in hand when you get on the call, so the call can be spent on the answers that surprised you and not on reading questions aloud.

Keep it to one page and tell her how long it takes. Ten to fifteen minutes is honest for the list below. A form that looks like homework does not get filled in.

Section 1: The Basics (4 Questions)

These feel obvious, which is why they get skipped and then cause the first awkward moment.

  1. Legal business name and the name you go by day to day. The first goes on the invoice. The second goes in every message.
  2. Who receives and pays invoices, and at what email address? Often not the person who hired you.
  3. Your time zone and normal working hours. You need this before you promise a turnaround time.
  4. Is anyone else on your team someone I will work with or take direction from? If yes, name and role. This is the question that prevents two people handing you conflicting priorities.

Section 2: Communication (4 Questions)

  1. Where do you want day-to-day messages: email, Slack, text, something else? Pick one. A client who messages you in four places will lose a request in one of them and remember it as you dropping it.
  2. How quickly do you expect a reply during working hours? Whatever she writes, you now have a number to agree to or to adjust on the kickoff call. Without the question, the number still exists. You just do not know it.
  3. What counts as urgent, and how should I reach you when something is? Ask for one or two real examples.
  4. How do you like to receive updates: a weekly summary, a shared tracker, a short call? This sets up the reporting habit that makes you hard to cancel later.

Section 3: The Work (5 Questions)

  1. What are the three tasks you most want off your plate in the first 30 days? Three, not ten. This becomes your plan for the first month.
  2. For each one, what does done look like? "Inbox managed" means something different to every client. Make her describe it.
  3. Is there a process, document or past example I should follow? If there is, you save a week. If there is not, you now know you are building it, and that is worth saying out loud.
  4. What has gone wrong with this work before? The most useful question on the form. The answer tells you what she is actually worried about, and it is rarely what the job description said.
  5. Is there anything you want to approve before it goes out? Get the approval line drawn now. Posts, client emails, anything involving money.

Section 4: Tools and Access (3 Questions)

  1. Which tools will I be working in? Ask for a list, not a description.
  2. How will you share access: a password manager, delegated access, a separate login for me? Do not accept passwords in an email or a text. Here is the safe way to handle client logins.
  3. Are there accounts or folders I should not touch? Clients rarely volunteer this and always have an answer.

Section 5: Boundaries (2 Questions)

  1. Are there dates in the next three months when you will be unreachable? You will need decisions from her. Know when you cannot get them.
  2. Is there anything else I should know to work well with you? An open box at the end. About half the time it is empty. The other half it holds the single most important sentence on the form.

What to Leave Off

Anything you already covered on the discovery call or in the contract. Do not ask about budget, rate or hours here. Those were agreed before she signed, and asking again reads as if you were not listening. Do not ask about her five-year vision either. It is a nice conversation and it does not help you clear her inbox on Monday.

Also leave off anything you will not use. Every question is a small cost to the client. If you cannot say what you would do differently based on the answer, cut it.

What to Do With the Answers

Read the form the day it comes back and do three things. Put the invoice contact, time zone and reply-time expectation somewhere you will see them every week. Turn the three priority tasks and their definitions of done into the first section of your scope of work if they are not there already. And write down the two answers you want to talk about on the kickoff call, usually the one about what went wrong before and the one about approvals.

If an answer contradicts the contract, say more hours than you agreed or a reply time you cannot meet, raise it on the kickoff call and not in week three. That one conversation is the whole reason the form goes out first.

Build It Once

A Google Form works. So does a shared doc with the questions typed out. What matters is that it is the same form for every client, so you are not rewriting it at 9 p.m. the night someone signs.
