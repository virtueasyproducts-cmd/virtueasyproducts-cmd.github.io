How to Build Your First VA Prospect List (50 Businesses Worth Pitching and a Simple Way to Track Them)

2 November 2026 · Virtueasy · Prospecting · 7 min read

Most VAs don't have a client problem. They have a list problem.

If your outreach has felt random, scattered, or like you are just throwing messages at whoever happened to post in a Facebook group that morning, this is the step you skipped. Before you write a single pitch, before you send one cold DM, you need a prospect list. Not a vague idea of who you want to work with. An actual document with actual names.

This post shows you how to build one: where to find 50 real businesses worth pitching, what to track for each one, and how to keep it running on a simple spreadsheet with no paid tools required.

Related: How to find your first VA client

Who Belongs on Your List

Before you open a spreadsheet or start searching, answer one question: what problem do you solve, and for what kind of business?

Not every business is a good fit. A bookkeeper who hates social media is not your prospect if your offer is content scheduling. A solo realtor who posts twice a day is not your prospect if your offer is inbox management.

Your list should reflect your offer. Pick one type of business and one type of problem. That focus is what keeps your outreach from sounding generic, and generic pitches get ignored.

If you are not clear on your one sentence offer yet, nail that down before you build this list.

Five Places to Find 50 Names

These five sources can get you to 50 names in one focused afternoon. You do not need all five. You need to actually open them and start writing names down.

1. Local Business Directories and Chambers of Commerce

Your city or region almost certainly has a chamber of commerce website with a member directory. Many local business associations do too. These lists are public, the businesses are real, and they are often small enough that you would be emailing the owner directly. Search for your city name plus "chamber of commerce directory" or "small business association members."

2. Niche Facebook Groups and LinkedIn

Join two or three groups where your ideal client hangs out. Not VA groups: their groups. A group for real estate agents, a group for online coaches, a group for bookkeepers. Watch who is posting about being overwhelmed, who is asking for recommendations for help, and who mentions doing everything themselves. LinkedIn works the same way. Search job titles, look at who is active, and check their company size.

3. Podcast Guest Lists and Event Speaker Pages

Find two or three podcasts your ideal client listens to or appears on. The guest list is a ready-made directory of people in that niche who are visible enough to be building something. Same with speaker pages from online summits and industry events. These people have an audience, which usually means they have a business with real demand on their time.

4. Businesses Visibly Hiring for Part-Time Admin Help

A job post for a part-time executive assistant or virtual admin is one of the clearest signals that a business needs help right now. Check Indeed, LinkedIn Jobs, and local Facebook groups. A business that is actively hiring already knows it has a capacity problem. That is a warm lead.

5. Your Warm Network

People you already know. Former coworkers, people from church, people you follow on Instagram who run a small business, the dentist your family uses, the photographer who shot your cousin's wedding. You do not have to pitch them cold. You just have to remember they exist and put them on the list. Warm outreach usually converts better than cold.

Signals a Business Is Ready for Help Right Now

Not every name on your list is equally worth your time. As you research each one, look for signs they are already feeling the pressure.

  • Slow replies. If their Google reviews mention slow response times, or their social comments go unanswered for days, someone is dropping the ball on communication.
  • Stale social media. A business whose last post was three months ago is not absent by choice. They ran out of time. That is an opening.
  • A job post for admin help. Covered above. It is a green light.
  • A new location, product launch, or service expansion. Growth creates chaos, and chaos creates demand for support.
  • One person wearing every hat. Look at their website, their About page, their LinkedIn. If it is clearly a solo operator doing everything, they are a candidate.

What to Capture for Each Row

Your spreadsheet does not need to be complicated. One row per prospect, six columns:

  • Name. The person's name, not the business name.
  • Business. What they do and roughly how big they appear to be.
  • Where you found them. The directory, the group, the job post. This matters for your opening line later.
  • One specific observation. Something you actually noticed. Their last post was about being overwhelmed. Their Google listing has not been updated since last year. They just announced a new offer. This is what makes your outreach sound like a human wrote it.
  • Status. A simple label: new, contacted, replied, not interested, follow up.
  • Next follow-up date. Leave it blank until you have made contact. Then fill it in so nothing falls through.

That is it. Six columns. You can build this in Google Sheets right now for free.

The Goal: 50 Names in One Afternoon, Ten a Week After That

Fifty feels like a lot until you remember that most of your outreach will not get a reply, and that is normal. Having 50 names means you are not depending on any one person saying yes. It also means you can choose who to contact first instead of contacting whoever comes to mind.

Spend one focused afternoon this week on nothing but building the list. Set a timer. Open the five sources above. Do not pitch anyone yet. Find names, put them in the spreadsheet, add one observation per row, and move on.

After that, make it a weekly habit. Ten new names a week takes less than an hour and keeps your pipeline from drying up after your first few outreach attempts.

What Comes Next

The list is the foundation. Once you have it, you know who to contact, you have something specific to say to each person, and your outreach stops feeling like guesswork.

The next step is writing the actual first message. That is a different skill, and a different post.
