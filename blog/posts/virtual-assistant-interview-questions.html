Virtual Assistant Interview Questions (and How to Answer Them)

23 September 2026 · Virtueasy · Job Hunting · 8 min read

Your application got a reply and now there is a call on the calendar. Here is what they are going to ask, and what they are really trying to find out.

Getting the interview is the hard part. Most VA applications never get a reply, so if you are reading this with a call booked, you are already past the filter most people never clear.

The interview itself is more predictable than it feels. Clients hiring a virtual assistant tend to ask the same handful of questions, because they are all worried about the same few things: will you disappear, will you need hand-holding, will you handle their stuff carefully, and will you cost more than you are worth. Every question below is one of those worries wearing a different outfit.

"Tell me about yourself."

What it is really testing: whether you can be concise and relevant. They are picturing you writing their emails.

Do not walk through your life story. Give them three sentences: what you do, who you do it for, and one specific result. Something like: "I'm a virtual assistant focused on inbox and calendar management for small business owners. I currently support two clients, and for one of them I took over scheduling across three team calendars so she stopped double-booking. I'm looking for one more ongoing client in the 10 to 15 hour a week range."

If you are new and do not have client results yet, use a result from a previous job that maps to the work. Organizing a shared drive, running a front desk calendar, or keeping a volunteer group on schedule all count.

"Why do you want to work as a virtual assistant?"

What it is really testing: whether you will still be here in three months.

Clients have been burned by VAs who treated the role as a stopgap. You do not need a passionate answer. You need a stable one. "I want work I can do well on a schedule I control, and I've built my week around doing this long term" is honest and reassuring. Avoid anything that sounds like you are between real jobs.

"What tools are you comfortable with?"

What it is really testing: how much they will have to teach you.

Name the tools you actually use, then say how you learn new ones. "I use Google Workspace, Asana and Canva daily. If you're on something I haven't used, I'll learn it on my own time before I start, not on your clock." That last sentence does more work than a long list of software.

Do not claim tools you have only opened once. It will come out in the first week.

"How do you handle confidential information and logins?"

What it is really testing: whether they can trust you with access to their business.

Be specific. Say you use a password manager and prefer shared vault access over passwords sent by email or text, that you use two-factor authentication on everything, and that you are happy to sign an NDA. Specific habits beat "I'm very trustworthy" every time.

"Tell me about a time something went wrong."

What it is really testing: whether you tell them about problems or hide them.

Pick a real, small mistake. Say what happened, what you did to fix it, and what you changed so it would not happen again. The fix and the change matter more than the mistake. A client would much rather hire someone who says "I caught it, told them the same day, and now I double-check time zones on every invite" than someone who claims nothing has ever gone wrong.

"How do you manage your time with more than one client?"

What it is really testing: whether their work will get dropped when someone else gets loud.

Describe your actual system: set hours or blocks per client, a response time you commit to, and how you track tasks. "I reply to messages within one business day, usually the same day, and I block your hours on my calendar so they don't get eaten by other work" answers the worry directly.

"What is your rate?"

What it is really testing: whether you know your worth, and whether there is room to negotiate.

Have a number ready before the call and say it plainly. Hesitating or saying "whatever you think is fair" tells them you will accept less. If the role is ongoing, you can offer a monthly package instead of an hourly rate, which gives them a predictable cost and gives you a predictable income.

If you are not sure what to charge, work it out before the call, not during it. Guessing live is how VAs end up locked into a rate they resent by month two.

"Would you be willing to do a test task?"

What it is really testing: sometimes your skills, sometimes whether you will work for free.

A short, paid trial is reasonable. An unpaid project that looks suspiciously like real work is not. You can say: "Happy to do a paid trial. I'd suggest a few hours at my standard rate so you can see how I work on something real." Clients who are serious will usually agree. Clients who push back on paying for a trial have told you how the rest of the relationship will go.

The Three Questions You Should Ask Back

An interview goes both ways, and asking good questions makes you look more experienced, not less. At minimum, ask these:

  • "What does a successful first month look like to you?" If they cannot answer, the goalposts will move later.
  • "Who else will I be taking direction from?" Invisible stakeholders are where scope creep starts.
  • "What happened with the last person in this role?" The way they answer tells you a lot about how they will talk about you.

Before You Hang Up

Close the call by confirming next steps. "What's your timeline for deciding, and is there anything else you need from me?" Then send a short follow-up email the same day that repeats your rate, your availability, and one thing you heard them say they need help with. That email is often what tips the decision, because most applicants never send one.
