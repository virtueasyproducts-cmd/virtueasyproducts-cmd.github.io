How to Write a Virtual Assistant Resume (When the Listing Asks for One)

16 October 2026 · Virtueasy · Job Hunting · 7 min read

Most freelance clients never ask for a resume. But plenty of remote VA roles do, and the one you send is usually read in a quick scan by someone with fifty more to get through.

If you are pitching a small business owner directly, skip the resume. A short, specific message beats it every time. But a lot of remote VA openings, especially ongoing part-time and full-time roles posted on job boards, have an application form with a resume upload. Leave it empty and you are out before anyone reads your message.

So you need one. Not a long one, and not the one you used for your last office job. A VA resume has one job: show the person hiring that you can do this specific work, remotely, without being managed closely.

What Goes at the Top

The top third of the page decides whether the rest gets read. Put these there, in this order:

  • Your name and the role. "Executive Virtual Assistant" or "Social Media VA", matched to the listing. Not "Hardworking professional seeking opportunities."
  • Your working hours in the client's time zone. "Available 9am to 5pm US Eastern" answers a question every overseas hire is silently asking. If you are in the Philippines or Pakistan applying for US roles, this one line matters more than anything else on the page.
  • Email and one link. A portfolio, a LinkedIn profile, or a shared folder of samples. One link that opens without a permission request.
  • A two line summary. What you do, for whom, and one concrete result. "Admin VA for small service businesses. Took over inbox and scheduling for a three person agency and cut their reply time from two days to same day."

You do not need a photo, your age, your marital status or your full street address. City and country is enough.

Turn Past Jobs Into VA Experience

You probably have more relevant experience than you think. Retail, call centers, teaching, reception, office admin and customer service all involve the same tasks clients hire VAs for. The trick is to describe the task, not the job title.

Before: "Front desk receptionist, 2021 to 2024. Answered phones and greeted customers."

After: "Front desk receptionist, 2021 to 2024. Managed a shared calendar for four providers, booked and rescheduled around 40 appointments a day, and handled billing questions by phone and email."

The second version is the same job. It just names the work a client would pay a VA to do: calendar management, scheduling, customer email. Use real numbers where you have them. Estimates are fine if they are honest. Invented ones fall apart in the interview.

Write each line as a short bullet that starts with what you did: managed, scheduled, drafted, organized, tracked, resolved. Three or four bullets per role is plenty.

The Skills and Tools Section

List the tools you can actually use today, grouped so they are easy to scan:

  • Email and calendar: Gmail, Outlook, Google Calendar, Calendly
  • Documents and data: Google Docs and Sheets, Excel, Word
  • Projects and communication: Trello, Asana, Slack, Zoom
  • Anything role specific: Canva for social roles, QuickBooks for bookkeeping, a CRM if the listing names one

Only list a tool if you could open it tomorrow and start working. "Familiar with" ten platforms reads as padding. Five you use well reads as someone who can start on Monday.

If You Have No VA Experience Yet

Add a short "Projects" section above your work history. One or two practice pieces you made yourself count: a cleaned up sample spreadsheet, a week of drafted social posts for a made up bakery, an inbox sorting system you documented in a Google Doc. Label them honestly as sample projects. Clients would rather see real work you did for practice than a claim you cannot back up.

This is also where a portfolio link earns its place at the top of the page. A resume says you can do the work. A sample shows it.

Mistakes That Get a Resume Skipped

  • Sending the same resume to every listing. Change the role title and summary to match each post. It takes ten minutes and it is the difference between "maybe" and "next."
  • Going over two pages. One page is better. Nobody needs your high school job.
  • Typos. Attention to detail is the core skill being hired. A spelling mistake on the resume answers that question for them.
  • A file called resume.pdf. Name it with your name and the role, like Maria-Santos-Admin-VA.pdf, so it does not get lost in a download folder.
  • Ignoring the listing's own words. If the post says "inbox management" and "client communication", use those exact phrases where they are true. Some companies filter applications by keyword before a person ever sees them.
