How to Write Your VA Offer in One Sentence

13 October 2026 · Virtueasy · Positioning · 6 min read

You can do a lot of things. That is the problem. A list of services reads as uncertainty, and one clear sentence tells the right prospect exactly what to hire you for.

When a prospect asks what you do and you say "I help with admin, inbox management, social media, scheduling, data entry, and a few other things," they do not think "great, this person can do everything." They think "this person does not know what they do yet." And then they move on.

This post is about fixing that, not by picking fewer skills, but by packaging what you do into one sentence that makes a specific person say "that is exactly what I need."

Why a List Reads as Uncertainty

A list of services feels safe when you are starting out. You do not want to close doors. You are not sure yet what clients will pay for, and you can technically do all of it, so why not offer all of it?

Here is what happens on the other end. A prospect, someone running a small business with limited time and limited trust in strangers on the internet, reads your list and has to do the work of figuring out whether you are relevant to them. That is effort they will not spend. They will move to the next person.

A one-sentence offer removes that work. It tells them immediately: this person is for me, or this person is not. Either outcome is better than confusion.

The Formula

Your one-sentence VA offer has three parts.

Who you help. Be specific enough that the right person recognizes themselves. "Small business owners" is not specific. "Solo real estate agents" is. "Local service businesses without a marketing team" is.

What you take off their plate. One task or a tight cluster of tasks, stated plainly. Not "support" or "management." The actual thing: inbox sorted and answered daily, social posts scheduled a week ahead, receipts categorized and sent to their accountant every month.

Why it is low risk to say yes. This does not have to be a guarantee. It can be a timeframe, a format, or a boundary that makes the ask feel small. "Starting with a two-week trial," or "month to month, no contract," or "I send a summary every Friday so you always know what has been handled."

Put it together: I help [who] [do or stop doing something specific] so they [outcome], starting with [low-friction entry point].

Three Weak Offers, Rewritten

Weak: "I'm a VA and I do inbox and calendar management for busy professionals."
Strong: "I handle inbox triage and calendar blocking for solo real estate agents who are losing deals because they can't keep up with follow-up, and I start with a two-week paid trial so you can see how it works before committing."

What changed: the prospect went from "busy professionals" (anyone) to "solo real estate agents losing deals to follow-up gaps" (a person with a specific, painful problem). The trial removes the risk of saying yes.

Weak: "I offer social media management for small businesses."
Strong: "I create and schedule one week of Instagram content at a time for local bakeries that want to post consistently but don't have time to sit down and do it themselves."

What changed: one week of content at a time feels manageable to offer and easy to evaluate. "Local bakeries" is small enough to be a real community.

Weak: "I do bookkeeping and admin support for entrepreneurs."
Strong: "I collect, sort, and send categorized receipts and invoices to your accountant each month so small contractors aren't scrambling at tax time."

What changed: the task is completely concrete. The outcome, not scrambling at tax time, is something every small contractor has felt. There is no ambiguity about what they are buying.

Why Narrowing Feels Scary and Costs Less Than It Seems

If you say you work with real estate agents, you will hear from real estate agents. You will also still be hired by people adjacent to that world, because specificity signals competence. A prospect who runs a small law firm sees "solo real estate agents" and thinks "this person knows how to handle a high-volume professional inbox," which is exactly their problem too.

Narrowing your stated offer does not restrict who you can work with. It changes how you read to someone who does not know you yet. That is the only thing the sentence is doing.

The cost of staying vague is higher. Vague offers produce vague inquiries, scope creep, and clients who do not know what they hired you to do. A tight sentence attracts people who already understand the value before they get on a call with you.

Test It Before You Post It Anywhere

Find one person who fits your target description. It can be someone you know, a contact on LinkedIn, or a person in a local business group. Tell them your sentence and ask two questions: "Does that make sense?" and "Is there anything in there you'd want me to explain?"

You are not asking if they want to hire you. You are checking whether the sentence lands. If they ask a clarifying question, your sentence needs work. If they say "oh, so you basically handle X," and X is what you meant, you are done.

One conversation. That is the test.

Where the Finished Sentence Goes

Once you have a sentence that passes the one-person test, it goes in four places.

  • Your profile headline. LinkedIn, your website, any VA directory you use. The headline is one sentence, not a list of skills.
  • The first line of every cold DM or outreach email. Not "Hi, my name is..." but the offer. Lead with what you do for someone like them.
  • The top of your proposal or services document. Before pricing, before packages, before your background. One sentence that confirms you understood what they need.
  • Your answer to "so what do you do?" At a networking event, on a call, anywhere. You have one sentence now. Use it.

A Template You Can Fill In Before You Close This Tab

"I help [specific type of person] [handle a specific task or stop dealing with a specific problem] so they [outcome], starting with [low-risk entry point]."

Fill in the brackets. Read it out loud. If it sounds like something a real person would say, you are done. If it sounds like a LinkedIn bio from 2019, cut a word and try again.

The sentence is not your whole business. It is the door. It gets someone curious enough to ask the next question, and that is all it needs to do.
