Where to Find Legitimate VA Jobs: Job Boards, Freelance Platforms, Communities and Direct Pitching Compared

18 November 2026 · Virtueasy · Finding Clients · 7 min read

You can spend three hours a day searching for VA work and still end up with nothing worth applying to. The problem usually is not effort. It is searching in the wrong places, or spreading yourself across five platforms when two would do the job.

Everyone says "just get on Upwork." Here is what they leave out.

This post compares the five main sources of VA work. For each one you will get a straight answer on who it actually suits, what rate level to expect, how much scam risk exists, and roughly how many minutes a week it deserves. At the end, there is a simple weekly search routine you can start using today.

Before anything else: if a listing looks off to you, trust that instinct. The signs are consistent and learnable.

Niche VA Job Boards

This is the category most beginners scroll past because it sounds small. It is not.

Niche VA job boards, including the Virtueasy VA Job Board, list work posted specifically for virtual assistants. The businesses posting there already understand what a VA does. You are not explaining the role from scratch or competing against applicants from completely unrelated fields.

Who it suits: Any VA, but especially someone looking for a first client or filling a gap after a retainer ends. The listings tend to be more specific about scope, which makes it easier to judge fit before you apply.

Rate level: Variable, but the floor tends to be higher than general freelance platforms because the clients are self-selected. They came to a VA-specific board on purpose.

Scam risk: Lower than general job boards, though not zero. Any board that does not vet listings can carry bad actors. Check for a named business, a real web presence, and a clear scope of work before you apply.

Time per week: 10 to 15 minutes scanning for new posts. If a board sends email alerts, set one up and let it come to you.

General Freelance Platforms (Upwork and Fiverr)

These platforms have real work on them. They also have real problems, and new VAs often find out about those problems after spending significant time building a profile that gets no traction.

Upwork runs on proposals. You apply, clients review, and the platform takes a service fee from your earnings. Competition is high, rates on entry-level listings can be low, and building enough reviews to command decent rates takes time. That said, Upwork does have legitimate mid-range clients who want longer-term VA relationships. The platform suits a VA who has at least a few months of documented experience and is willing to invest time in a strong profile before expecting results.

Fiverr works differently. You list a service, clients come to you. This is useful if you have a specific, packageable skill (email management, calendar coordination, research). It is harder to build a stable retainer relationship through Fiverr and easier to end up doing low-ticket one-off tasks indefinitely.

Who it suits: Upwork is better if you want ongoing client relationships and can wait out the early slow period. Fiverr suits a narrow, defined offer.

Rate level: Entry rates are frequently below market. It is possible to raise them, but it takes reviews and time.

Scam risk: Moderate. Both platforms have verification processes, but neither eliminates bad clients entirely. Never move communication off-platform before a contract is signed.

Time per week: 20 to 30 minutes on Upwork if you are actively building there. Set Fiverr up properly once and monitor it rather than hunting for work on it daily.

Remote Job Boards (FlexJobs, Remote.co, We Work Remotely)

These boards list remote roles across many industries. VA work appears here, but so does everything else. FlexJobs charges a subscription fee and manually screens listings for legitimacy, which is worth something. Remote.co and We Work Remotely are free to search, though the VA-specific volume is lower.

Who it suits: VAs open to part-time employee roles or longer contracts with established companies, rather than freelance project work. The listings here often want someone who will work set hours.

Rate level: Generally fair. Companies posting on these boards are usually established enough to have a real budget.

Scam risk: Low on paid and curated boards like FlexJobs. Moderate on free boards. The same rules apply: named company, verifiable web presence, clear job description.

Time per week: 10 minutes, two or three times a week. Set keyword alerts where the platform allows it.

Facebook Groups and Slack Communities

This is where a lot of unadvertised VA work actually moves. Business owners post in groups before they ever list a job formally. Members refer each other. Rates are sometimes negotiated directly and without platform fees.

The catch is signal-to-noise ratio. Groups vary in quality. Some are well-moderated and genuinely useful. Others are full of low-budget posts and multi-level recruitment attempts. Spend a week observing a group before engaging, and check the rules before posting.

Slack communities built around specific industries (online business, e-commerce, content creators) can surface better-fit clients than general VA groups, because you can position yourself as someone who understands their specific context.

Who it suits: VAs who are comfortable with informal outreach and want to build relationships rather than just submit applications. Also useful for getting referrals once you have a track record.

Rate level: Varies widely. No floor unless you set one.

Scam risk: Higher than curated boards. Use the same vetting approach: named person, real business presence, scope that makes sense for the ask.

Time per week: 10 to 15 minutes daily if you are active in a few targeted groups. Passive monitoring once you are established.

Direct Pitching

This is the approach most new VAs put off because it feels harder. It is also the approach that bypasses every platform, every fee, and most of the competition.

Direct pitching means identifying a business that could use VA support, finding the right contact, and sending a brief, specific message about how you can help. It is not a cold sales email. It is a short, relevant note that shows you understand their business and have something specific to offer.

The businesses worth approaching are ones where you can see a gap: a newsletter that goes out inconsistently, a social presence that stops and starts, a podcast with no show notes. You are not guessing. You are observing.

Who it suits: VAs who have at least one area of demonstrated strength and can describe it clearly. It is especially useful for replacing a dropped retainer quickly, because you can target businesses similar to the one you just lost.

Rate level: You set it. No platform fee, no race to the bottom.

Scam risk: Near zero. You chose the target.

Time per week: 20 to 30 minutes identifying two or three targets and writing or refining your outreach.

A 30 Minute Weekly Search Routine

Monday, 10 minutes: Scan the Virtueasy VA Job Board and any remote board alerts that came in. Flag anything worth a closer look.

Wednesday, 10 minutes: Check your active Facebook or Slack communities for new posts. Reply to two or three if you have something useful to contribute.

Friday, 10 minutes: Research one or two direct pitch targets. If you already have a template, refine it for the specific business. If you do not have one yet, write the first draft.

On applications: apply to no more than five listings per week, but make each one specific. A tailored application to a listing that actually fits you will outperform ten generic ones every time.

The Honest Summary

No single source will fill your calendar on its own. A niche VA board plus one community plus one round of direct pitches per week covers more ground than refreshing a general job board for an hour every morning. Work the sources that match where you are right now, and adjust as your business grows.
