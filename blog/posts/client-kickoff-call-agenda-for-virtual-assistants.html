Your First Client Kickoff Call: A 45-Minute Agenda

8 September 2026 · Virtueasy · Onboarding · 6 min read

The meeting after they say yes is the one most VAs completely wing. Here is the 45-minute agenda to run instead.

You signed the client. The contract is done. Now there is one meeting standing between you and the actual work, and most virtual assistants either skip it entirely or spend 45 minutes on small talk and vague excitement.

That is how retainers fall apart in month two.

The kickoff call is not a formality. It is the single conversation where you either get the information you need to work cleanly for the next six months, or you spend the rest of the engagement guessing, chasing, and apologizing. How you run this meeting tells the client exactly what kind of professional they hired.

Here is how to run it well.

Before the Call: Send a Prep Note

Twenty-four hours before the meeting, send the client a short message. Tell them what you will cover, how long it will take, and ask them to have one thing ready: a rough list of the tasks or projects they want to hand off first.

You are not asking for a full brief. You just want them thinking about priorities before they sit down with you. It makes the conversation faster and more honest.

The 45-Minute Agenda

Block your time into four segments. You do not need to share the timer with the client, but you should be tracking it.

Minutes 0 to 5: Open with context, not small talk

Start by stating the purpose of the call out loud. Something like: "The goal today is to make sure we both leave with a clear picture of what we are working on, how decisions get made, and how we communicate. By the end I am going to read back the scope so we are both on the same page before anything starts."

That one sentence signals structure. It also signals that you are not there to fill time.

Minutes 5 to 20: Goals and definition of done

This is where most kickoffs go wrong. VAs take the task list at face value without asking what a good outcome actually looks like.

Ask these questions:

  • What does success look like at the 90-day mark?
  • If we get to the end of month one and you feel like this is working, what would have happened?
  • Is there anything you have tried before with a VA or contractor that did not work the way you hoped?

That last question is worth its weight. Clients rarely volunteer what went wrong with the last person. When you ask directly, they usually tell you. That information protects you.

For each deliverable, agree on what done means. Not "manage my inbox" but "inbox is at zero every weekday by 5pm and anything requiring your response is flagged and summarized." Specificity here is client onboarding strategy in practice, not theory.

Minutes 20 to 35: How work actually moves

This section covers the operational reality of the retainer. Go through each of these points:

  • Approval path. Who approves what, and who is the backup if that person is unavailable? Some clients want to sign off on everything. Others want you to act and report. Find out which one you have.
  • Where files live. Confirm the tools before the call ends. Google Drive, Notion, a shared inbox, a project management tool. If the client does not have a system, this is the moment to propose one. Do not leave it open.
  • Response time expectations, in both directions. How quickly do they expect to hear from you? How quickly should you expect to hear back from them? This is the conversation that prevents the "I sent three messages and heard nothing for a week" complaint later.
  • What happens when priorities change. Retainer clients will shift focus, sometimes mid-week. Ask how they want to handle that. Do they send a message flagged urgent? Do you have a standing check-in where you reorder the list? Agree on the process now.
  • Communication channel. Pick one primary channel for work requests. One. If they are going to text, email, Slack, and tag you in comments simultaneously, tasks will fall through the gaps. Set the norm here.

Minutes 35 to 45: Restate the scope out loud

This is the part most VAs skip because it feels awkward. Do it anyway.

Read back what you heard. Cover the deliverables, the volume or frequency, the tools you will use, the communication expectations, and any constraints. Do it in plain language, not bullet points.

Then ask: "Does that match what you are expecting, or did I miss anything?"

You will almost always surface at least one misalignment. Better here than after you have already built the wrong thing for two weeks.

Close by telling them what happens next. When will they get a summary of the call? When does work start? What do you need from them to begin? Name a specific date.

The Follow-Up Within 24 Hours

Send a written summary of the call the same day or the next morning. Not a formal document. A clear email or shared note that covers:

  • The agreed deliverables and what done looks like for each
  • Communication channel and expected response times
  • Where files and work will live
  • The first task or project you are starting with
  • Any open questions that still need answers

This document becomes your reference point when priorities shift or scope creeps. It is also proof that you ran a professional kickoff. Both matter.

What to Do When the Client Is Unprepared

Some clients will show up to the kickoff without having thought about any of this. They are busy. They hired you so they could stop thinking about it.

Do not let the meeting become a rambling brainstorm. Come with your own version of their goals based on what you know from the sales conversation, and ask them to confirm or correct it. "Based on what we discussed before signing, it sounds like the main focus is X and Y. Is that still accurate, or has anything shifted?"

That framing moves the conversation forward without making them feel put on the spot.

One Thing That Will Make Every Retainer Easier

Establish a weekly or biweekly check-in from day one. Even if it is 15 minutes. Even if the retainer is small.

Retainer client management breaks down when problems compound quietly. A short standing touchpoint means misalignments get caught early instead of turning into a difficult conversation in month three.

If they push back on a check-in, offer to make it async. A short written update they can read and reply to when it suits them. The format matters less than the habit.

The Meeting That Decides Everything

A well-run kickoff is one of the clearest demonstrations of kickoff call best practices you can show a new client. Not because it is impressive, but because it is useful. It tells them you know what you are doing and that working with you is going to be organized.

That first impression compounds. Clients who feel confident in you from week one are easier to work with, more likely to renew, and more likely to refer.

Take the 45 minutes seriously. Run the agenda. Read back the scope. Send the follow-up.

The work goes better when the foundation is solid.
